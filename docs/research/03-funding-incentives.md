# Funding: why an IEP is a cost, not revenue

**As of 2026-10-13.** Question asked: don't schools get funding per IEP, so wouldn't they want more IEPs if the process were easier? In California, no.

## California funds special education per enrolled student, not per IEP

- Since 1997 the main state formula (AB 602) has funded each SELPA by the average daily attendance of the whole student body, not the number of students with disabilities ([CSBA, 2018](https://www.csba.org/Newsroom/CSBANewsletters/2018/March/ElectronicOnly/2018_03_07spedfunding); [LAO, 2013](https://lao.ca.gov/handouts/education/2013/Special-Ed-Budget-Proposals050713.pdf)).
- That was deliberate: distributing funds by census counts of all students avoids creating financial incentives to identify students for special education ([PPIC, Special Education Finance in California](https://www.ppic.org/publication/special-education-finance-in-california/)).
- Going from 100 IEPs to 120 doesn't change the district's state allocation.

## Districts carry most of the cost

- Nearly $24 billion went to students with disabilities in 2024–25, just over 17% of school spending. State support covered 22% and federal 6%; districts covered the rest, rising from 63% in 2015 to 71% in 2025 ([PPIC, Oct 2026](https://www.ppic.org/wp-content/uploads/students-with-disabilities-in-california.pdf)).
- With enrollment declining and the count of students with disabilities rising, district budgets are under increasing pressure ([PPIC, Oct 2026](https://www.ppic.org/wp-content/uploads/students-with-disabilities-in-california.pdf)).
- Per-student special education spending rose 12% (inflation-adjusted) from 2005–06 to 2015–16 while per-student Prop 98 funding rose 2% ([CSBA, 2018](https://www.csba.org/Newsroom/CSBANewsletters/2018/March/ElectronicOnly/2018_03_07spedfunding)).

## The pull goes the other way, held back by law

Each new IEP adds staff time and services paid largely from the general budget. Child Find still requires identifying and assessing students with suspected disabilities, and getting it wrong is expensive (see [02](02-noncompliance-impacts.md)). `Hypothesis`: in practice the cost pressure shows up as delays and informal workarounds, like those in the two parent threads in [01](01-ca-iep-process.md#lessons-from-two-parent-threads).

## What this means for the product

- Don't pitch "more IEPs, more revenue." That money doesn't exist in California.
- Pitch cost and risk: fewer missed deadlines, lower legal exposure, less staff time per IEP, specialists freed for students already on caseload.
- Likely buyer: SELPA or district special education director, absorbing rising caseloads on flat budgets and carrying the compliance risk (see [04](04-buyer-selpa-director.md)).

## Caveats

- Some other states fund per identified student, sometimes weighted by disability, where the incentive you'd expect can be real. This file is California-only.
- Districts can bill Medi-Cal for some health-related IEP services. `Unverified`: how much that recovers wasn't researched.
